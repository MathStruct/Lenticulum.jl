Composing Optimisers

Composing Optimisers

09 Nov 202618 min read

  • design

design

The optimisers in this repository act on three kinds of state: parameters (OptimiserFactor, fit_em with SQUAREM), messages (a BP sweep, with damping) and, not yet, beliefs. Most proposals for faster or safer inference are modifiers of one of these maps: damping, momentum, Anderson or SQUAREM extrapolation, a mirror map, noise. This note asks how such pieces compose.

Two results. Fixed points compose; convergence does not. Each modifier keeps the base map’s fixed points, so any stack of them still satisfies condition (O) of The Regulariser and the Loss. But whether the stack converges, and how fast, depends on the spectrum of the linearised map, and two convergent pieces can compose into a divergent one. The spectrum also says which modifier to use: momentum where the slow eigenvalues are real and near 1, damping or Anderson where they are complex or near −1. That makes a spectral diagnostic the first tool to build, and a benchmark the way to decide the rest.

Measured on the divider graph (§4.1, bench/bp_spectrum.jl): every slow eigenvalue of a flooding sweep is complex, on a ring. Heavy ball diverges there for every β ≥ 0.07, damping gains at most 2% in rate, and the gain is in the schedule: one that goes round the loop in a sweep converges at ρ⁶ instead of ρ per sweep (38 sweeps instead of 326). A fixed order that goes once round the loop each way, or one built from a spanning tree with no knowledge of the loop, does that at flooding’s cost per sweep: about six times fewer message evaluations in every setting tried (§4.2, bench/bp_schedules.jl).

The second row is the one that matters most and is easiest to get wrong. A BP sweep is not a gradient step on F. It is a fixed-point map whose fixed points happen to be F‘s stationary points. So BP satisfies (O), but nothing guarantees it descends F on the way, and modifiers built for gradient descent (momentum, mirror maps) carry over only as fixed-point modifiers, with none of their descent guarantees.

The heavy-ball row in one line: a fixed point needs x′=x, so v′=0; then v′=v gives v=0, and αr(x)=0. The same argument as for Momentum in The Regulariser and the Loss §3, applied to a residual in place of a gradient.

The mirror rows apply only where T is a gradient step, so for BP they need the belief-row map of §1 first. Their use is geometric. With the log-partition as ψ, mirror descent in the mean parameters is natural-gradient descent in the natural ones (Raskutti & Mukherjee 2015), so every iterate is a valid belief. Leaving that set is what broke the scratch heavy-ball test (Feature Backlog, the Langevin row: a negative precision, then NaN).

3. Three ways to compose, and what each keeps

Wrapping (a modifier around a map, or a stack of them). Every modifier above except temperature and a learned rule keeps Fix(T), so a stack of them does too. Damping inside Anderson inside a schedule still satisfies (O). That is the good news, and it holds whatever the order.

Alternation on separate blocks (inference sweeps and parameter steps, as in EM or the proposed train!). If Tb​ updates the beliefs given θ and Tθ​ the parameters given b, the alternation (b,θ)↦(Tb​(b,θ),Tθ​(Tb​(b,θ),θ)) is at rest exactly when b=Tb​(b,θ) and θ=Tθ​(b,θ): the joint fixed points. When both blocks descend the same F (condition (I)), those are its stationary points. When they descend different objectives, the fixed points are a game’s equilibria instead (The Two-Part Diagram §3).

Nesting (an inner solve to a tolerance inside each outer step). The outer fixed points are stationary only up to the inner tolerance, and the outer gradient is either unrolled through the inner iterations or taken at the inner fixed point by the implicit function theorem, which Mycelium already does (adjoint = :implicit in free_energy.jl). Inner tolerance becomes a hyperparameter of the composite.

What does not compose: convergence. Two maps that each converge can compose into one that diverges. For linear maps the rate is the spectral radius, and it is not submultiplicative:

J1​=(00​20​),J2​=(02​00​),ρ(J1​)=ρ(J2​)=0,ρ(J2​J1​)=4.

Each converges in two steps; alternating them diverges. There is one positive law: if T1​ and T2​ are contractions in the same norm with factors c1​,c2​ and share a fixed point, their composite contracts with c1​c2​. Loopy BP is not a contraction in general, which is why no stack of modifiers on it comes with a guarantee for free.

4. The spectrum decides which modifier helps

Near a fixed point every map is its linearisation, e′=Je. For a Gaussian graph the mean update is exactly linear once the precisions have converged, so J can be computed. Then each modifier acts on each eigenvalue λ of J separately:

  • relaxation maps λ↦1−α+αλ: it pulls everything towards 1, so it cures eigenvalues near −1 (oscillation) and complex ones, and slows those near +1;
  • heavy ball (with α=1) turns each λ into the two roots of μ2−(λ+β)μ+β=0. For real λ it is stable exactly when −1−2β<λ<1, so it never destabilises a convergent real spectrum. At λ=0.99 the best β=0.81 gives ∣μ∣=0.9, about 10.5 times fewer iterations than 0.99. At λ=−0.99, β=0.3 gives ∣μ∣=0.55;
  • but for complex λ momentum can destabilise a convergent map:

A BP sweep’s Jacobian is not symmetric, so complex eigenvalues are possible. Gradient descent on a smooth F has a symmetric Jacobian and real eigenvalues, which is the setting momentum was designed for. On the divider graph a sweep’s slow eigenvalues are all complex (§4.1), and the linear analysis alone predicts the momentum failure.

Anderson needs no such case split. It fits the best combination of the last m residuals, so on a linear map it is a Krylov method (GMRES-like) and handles complex spectra. That makes it the natural first accelerator for BP sweeps, and the cheapest to try: it wraps sweep! through AbstractSchedule and needs none of the belief-geometry additions.

4.1 Measured: the divider (experiment 1)

bench/bp_spectrum.jl linearises one flooding sweep of the resistive divider (test/runtests.jl: three variables, three constraints, one loop of six edges, weak priors of variance 104) at its fixed point, by central differences over every message’s (η,Λ). The precisions do not depend on the means (∂Λ′/∂η=0 exactly), so the spectrum is the mean block’s and the precision block’s together.

(12 nonzero eigenvalues in each block, all of the same modulus to 10−10; the error is the largest of the mean and precision errors, each relative to its block’s largest entry.)

  • All the slow eigenvalues are complex, on a ring. The mean eigenvalues sit at ±30°, ±90° and ±150°: the sixth roots of −ρ6. The precision eigenvalues sit at 0°, ±60°, ±120° and 180°, the sixth roots of +ρΛ6​, with ρΛ​=ρ2 in every case. So one trip round the six-edge loop takes six flooding sweeps, and a mean comes back multiplied by −ρ6. In the three unequal cases ρ6=Rmin​/Rmax​; with equal resistors it is 1−9.5⋅10−4. That is an observation on four settings, not derived. The test’s divider is a benign case of a graph that flooding solves badly.
  • The prediction is exact. Undamped, the measured asymptotic rate is 0.9346, 0.8324 and 0.9347, against ρ = 0.9347, 0.8327 and 0.9347.
  • Momentum is ruled out on this graph, not merely untuned. Heavy ball’s spectral radius on the test’s divider is 0.9864 at β=0.05, 0.9964 at 0.06, 1.0062 at 0.07 and 1.2043 at 0.3. With equal resistors it diverges for every β≥0.05. The scratch failure for every β≥0.3 (Feature Backlog, the Langevin row) is what the linear analysis predicts, before any precision goes negative.
  • Damping can barely help. Relaxation pulls a ring towards 1 and shrinks some eigenvalues at the expense of others: the best uniform α=0.75 gives 0.9260 against 0.9347. Mycelium’s damping relaxes only the factor-to-variable messages (passing.jl, _step!), so its Jacobian is DJ+(I−D) with D=1−damping on those messages and 1 elsewhere, not 1−α+αλ on every eigenvalue. That prediction matches the measured rate at damping 0.5 (0.9167 and 0.9164) and 0.8 (0.9591 and 0.9591). At 0.25, and at 0.5 for R=1,3, the runs converge faster than predicted, as a start that barely excites the leading modes would (not checked). And the rate is not the sweep count: damping 0.5 has the better rate (0.9167 against 0.9347) but needs 410 sweeps against 326, because the transients differ.
  • The schedule is the modifier that works. A residual schedule (the largest change first) converges at exactly ρ6 per sweep, a trip round the loop in each sweep: 38 sweeps instead of 326, 15 instead of 122. Its sweeps cost more, though: it evaluates every task’s candidate before each commit, 182 evaluations against flooding’s 18, so it needs about twice flooding’s message evaluations (12 312 against 5 868). Its order, replayed as a plain sequential sweep (with the tasks it skipped appended), keeps the rate at flooding’s cost per sweep for R=1,3 (462 evaluations against 2 196), partly for 2,3 (1 620 against 5 868), and not for 3,2. A sequential sweep in flooding’s task order gets ρ2 per sweep (two hops), at half flooding’s evaluations.

So on this graph the slow modes are a ring, momentum diverges, damping gains a few percent, and the gain is in the order. The expectation was ρ6 per sweep at flooding’s cost for a schedule that follows the loop; the replays above reach it on one of three settings, and §4.2 tests it directly. Anderson stays the open candidate: the mean block has six distinct nonzero eigenvalues, so on the linear part a short memory should already cancel them (an expectation, experiment 3).

4.2 Measured: the order of a sweep (experiment 2)

bench/bp_schedules.jl runs the same 18 messages of the divider six ways and counts message evaluations (calls of the update rule, committed or not) until the error against the fixed point falls below 10−10 (the error of §4.1, from a cold start):

  • flooding, double-buffered, 18 evaluations per sweep;
  • sequential, in place, in all_tasks order (edge by edge);
  • loop: the messages off the loop, then once round the loop each way (the cycle found by a depth-first search);
  • spanning: the messages off a spanning tree, then the tree’s inward and outward sweeps (_tree_sweeps from a variable; it needs no knowledge of the loop);
  • residual: ResidualSchedule, every candidate evaluated before each commit;
  • queue: residual BP with a priority queue (Elidan, McGraw & Koller, 2006): after a commit, only the messages that read the committed one are evaluated again (the dependencies of belief propagation; mean-field and EP nodes read more and are not covered).

  • A fixed order round the loop is the whole gain. Loop and spanning converge at ho6 per sweep (measured 0.6666 and 0.6667 for R=2,3; 0.3335 and 0.3326 for 1,3), the residual schedule’s rate, at flooding’s 18 evaluations per sweep: about six times fewer evaluations than flooding, in all four settings. The sequential sweep in edge order gets ho2 (0.8736), half.
  • The spanning-tree order needs no knowledge of the loop and matches the loop order to within 3%. It is the schedule to try first on graphs with several loops: every off-tree edge’s message is refreshed, then information crosses the whole tree in one sweep.
  • The priority queue removes the residual schedule’s quadratic cost (from 2.1 to 0.24 times flooding’s evaluations) but stays behind the static orders, at 0.24 to 0.31: its commits follow the loop like the residual schedule’s (690 commits, 38 sweeps’ worth, for R=2,3), and each commit costs about one more evaluation for its dependents. On this graph a fixed order is cheaper. Where the slow part of a graph is not known in advance, or moves, the queue is the adaptive option; that is untested.
  • With equal resistors flooding needs 97 224 sweeps, not the 145 000 that ho alone gives from an error of 1: the cold start puts little into the slowest modes (as for damping 0.25 in §4.1). The run’s rates over its last six sweeps are not asymptotic and are not reported; the fixed point is the loop order’s run to its rounding floor, and with 1−ρ6≈10−3 a residual at the floor leaves an error about a thousand times larger, so these counts are less certain than the others.

So the cheapest acceleration of BP on this graph is a change of schedule, no modifier at all: a spanning-tree order, a sixfold saving that keeps the fixed points (§3: a schedule changes the map, not its fixed points). Whether it holds on graphs with several loops, where no one tree carries every loop’s information in one sweep, is the next measurement (a Gaussian grid, in the benchmark).

5. The design space, and why it needs a benchmark

The choices multiply: the state (θ, m, b) × the schedule × the stack of modifiers × their hyperparameters (α, β, m, temperature) × nesting tolerances. Each claim so far was tried on one graph. Three consequences:

  1. A spectral diagnostic first. Report the leading eigenvalues of the linearised sweep at a fixed point (exact for Gaussian means; by finite differences or AD of sweep! otherwise). It says before any tuning whether momentum, damping or Anderson should help, and it can be checked against the observed iteration counts.
  2. A benchmark to compare stacks (Feature Backlog, Tooling): problems with reference answers where today’s methods are slow or fail, run by one script recording sweeps, wall time, error, the free energy reached and failures.
  3. A learned optimiser last. A learned Uψ​ trained on the benchmark can overfit it (its outer criterion is the benchmark), and must keep (O): the simplest way is to learn only the hyperparameters of a stack whose fixed points are fixed by construction (§3), not a free map.

6. Open questions

  1. What is a composite’s (O) when the pieces descend different objectives? Damping a sweep that minimises the Bethe F and an M-step that maximises a likelihood are both fine alone; the alternation’s fixed points are the joint stationary points only if the two objectives are one F (§3).
  2. Is there a norm in which BP contracts on a given graph? Where there is, composition laws become rates (§3); the walk-summable Gaussian graphs are the candidates.
  3. Do accelerations compose in continuous time? Wibisono et al. (2016) derive accelerated methods as discretisations of one Lagrangian flow. If the modifiers above are discretisations of flows, composing them is adding terms to the flow, and the question becomes which discretisation keeps the rate.
  4. Optimiser composition as lens composition. An update rule is a lens (Cruttwell et al., Def. 3.14), and wrapping is a map from lenses to lenses that keeps the fixed points. Whether the modifiers of §2 form a category acting on update lenses, with (O) as a property it preserves, is unworked.
