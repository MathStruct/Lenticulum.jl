Hierarchical Models

29 Oct 20267 min read

  • model
  • example

model example

A hierarchical model is an ordinary factor graph as long as the shared quantities are variables: the hyperprior is one more factor, attached to a shared variable, and it never touches a local factor’s parameters. Polarity gives both halves of partial pooling (shrinkage and pooling) from one factor. Only a hierarchy over the parameters of learned factors needs the wiring of Unusual Wirings. Three examples, each checked against a dense Gaussian.

Sources: Rubin, Estimation in Parallel Randomized Experiments, Journal of Educational Statistics 6(4), 1981 (the eight-schools data); Weiss & Freeman, Correctness of Belief Propagation in Gaussian Graphical Models of Arbitrary Topology, Neural Computation 13(10), 2001 (converged loopy Gaussian BP has exact means); the analysis is original to this vault. code: examples/hierarchical_models.jl, test/hierarchical_models.jl

Bibliography: Rubin 1981 · Weiss & Freeman 2001

Theory (CT-ML wiki): Bayesian Inversion · Statistical Game · Variational Free Energy

1. Where the hierarchy lives

A two-level model, groups j with observations i:

yij​∼p(y∣θj​),θj​∼p(θ∣ϕ),ϕ∼p(ϕ).

Nothing here is a new kind of edge. The factor p(θj​∣ϕ) is a relation between two variables like any other, and “long range” has no meaning in a factor graph: what makes it a hierarchy is only that many factors share the variable ϕ. The local factors are untouched; the hyperprior sees their channels θj​, never their parameters.

Which quantities are variables and which are parameters is the modelling choice that decides everything below:

the hierarchy runs overexamplewiringtoday
variables (means, effects, latent states)random intercepts; random slopes written with the weights as channelsordinary factor graphworks; exact on trees in the linear-Gaussian fragment (§2, §3)
variances of variablesθj​∼N(μ,τ2) with τ unknownordinary, but the factor p(θj​∣μ,τ) is not jointly Gaussianscan τ as a parameter (§2), or a non-Gaussian belief on τ (not in LenticulumCore)
parameters ps of learned factorsone network per group with a shared prior on the weights; a shared hyperprior on several factors’ noiseoutput into a weight, beliefs on weights (Unusual Wirings §2, §4)needs parameters exposed as channels (L-SPEC-003 Exposed Parameters and Energy Channels), not built

The third row turns into the first once the parameter is exposed: an exposed weight is a variable, and the hyperprior attaches to it as in the picture. Random slopes (§3) do that exposure by hand for a linear model.

2. Partial pooling is two polarities of one factor

The population factor p(θj​∣μ) sends two messages, and they are the two halves of partial pooling:

messagepolaritywhat it does
μ→θj​μ observed, θj​ unobservedshrinkage: the population acts as a prior on group j
θj​→μθj​ observed, μ unobservedpooling: each group’s evidence informs the population

On a tree (any two-level hierarchy whose groups meet only through μ) two sweeps give the exact posterior, and the Bethe Free Energy at the fixed point is −logp(y), the marginal likelihood used to choose the population variance.

The eight schools. Rubin’s (1981) coaching experiments: eight schools, an estimated effect yj​ with known standard error sj​ each. The model is yj​∼N(θj​,sj2​), θj​∼N(μ,τ2), μ∼N(0,1002). For each τ the graph is linear-Gaussian, and the posterior means (from examples/hierarchical_models.jl) move from the raw estimates towards the pooled mean as τ falls:

Schools with large standard errors (A, C, H) move furthest: their own evidence is weak, so the shrinkage message dominates. At every τ the means, variances and free energy agree with the dense Gaussian to 1.2⋅10−13.

Choosing τ. τ is the noise of the population factor, a parameter, so it is not inferred by message passing. Two ways to handle it, both shown in the example:

  • Type-II maximum likelihood minimises −logp(y∣τ), which the graph returns as its free energy (agreeing with the dense value to 6.4⋅10−14 over 160 values of τ). For these data the minimum is at the lower end of the scan: maximum likelihood pools the schools completely, a well-known weakness of plugging in a point estimate of τ.
  • A mixture over τ: with a uniform prior on a grid in [0.25,40], the weights ∝e−F(τ) give E[τ∣y]=6.61, and each θj​‘s posterior is a mixture of the per-τ Gaussians. School A comes out at 11.41±8.34, between complete pooling and its raw 28. The mixture is assembled outside the graph; inside it would be a belief on τ that is not Gaussian, the second row of the table in §1.

A new school. Add a ninth θ with a population factor and no data. Its marginal is the predictive effect of an unseen school: at τ=10, θ9​∼N(8.10,11.422), with μ∼N(8.10,5.512), so Varθ9​=Varμ+τ2. No new machinery: the same factor, queried with nothing on one side.

3. Random slopes: exposing the weights by hand

Group regressions yij​=βj1​+βj2​xij​+ε with βj​∼N(βˉ​,Σβ​). Written the usual way, βj​ would be the parameters of a regression factor, and a prior shared across groups would be the third row of §1. Written as a factor graph, the roles swap: the design matrix Xj​ is the parameter A of a GaussianFactor, and βj​ is its input channel, so the population factor βˉ​→βj​ attaches to it like to any variable. The graph is again a tree, and with five groups of six points the posterior (means, 2×2 covariances and −logp(y)) agrees with the dense Gaussian to 2.2⋅10−10.

For a linear model this rewrite is free. For a neural network it is the exposure that L-SPEC-003 Exposed Parameters and Energy Channels plans: the weights become a channel, the message to them is a likelihood, and it is no longer exact (a network is not linear in its weights; Unusual Wirings §2).

4. Crossed effects: hierarchy with loops

Not every hierarchy is a tree. Students r answering items c, with yrc​=ar​+bc​+ε and priors on both, put every observation on a row effect and a column effect: a full R×C design has (R−1)(C−1) independent loops. Each observation is one LinearConstraintFactor on (ar​,bc​) with the reading as its constant.

With R=4, C=3 (six loops) the flooding schedule converges, and:

  • the means agree with the exact posterior to 1.3⋅10−13, as Weiss & Freeman (2001) prove for any Gaussian graph on which belief propagation converges;
  • the variances do not: they are 0.60 to 0.65 of the exact ones, so the loopy posterior is overconfident here.

So crossed and multiple-membership hierarchies are expressible, with point estimates you can trust and uncertainties you cannot; Loopy Message Passing discusses remedies.

5. Summary

  • A hierarchy over variables is contained in implicit learning: the hyperprior is a factor, and partial pooling is two polarities of it.
  • Nested hierarchies are trees and exact in the linear-Gaussian fragment; crossed ones have loops, with exact means and approximate variances.
  • Unknown variances of the hierarchy are parameters today: scan them with the free energy, or mix over them outside the graph.
  • A hierarchy over the parameters of learned factors needs those parameters exposed as channels, which is planned but not built.
